El Pattern Day Trader Rule (PDT) establece que cualquier persona que realice 4 o más operaciones intradía en un período de 5 días hábiles es considerada pattern day trader. Para poder operar bajo esta condición en Estados Unidos, se exige un capital mínimo de $25,000 en la cuenta antes de iniciar las operaciones. Si el saldo baja de ese monto, no se permite seguir operando hasta reponerlo.

Esta regla, aplicada estrictamente por los brokers regulados por FINRA en EE.UU., busca proteger a los pequeños traders de pérdidas excesivas y comisiones altas.

Sin embargo, los brokers offshore (fuera de EE.UU.), como SureTrader (Bahamas), Capital Markets Elite Group (Trinidad y Tobago) y Alliance Trader (Jamaica), no están sujetos a esta regulación y permiten operar con cuentas menores a $25,000. A cambio, suelen aplicar comisiones más altas que los brokers estadounidenses.

brokers offshore = More commission

Los brokers offshore permiten evitar la regla PDT y operar con menos de $25,000, pero conllevan mayor riesgo. Aunque ofrecen más flexibilidad, no están regulados en EE.UU., por lo que muchos traders prefieren no mantener grandes cantidades de dinero en esas cuentas. Por ejemplo, se considera razonable tener entre $5,000 y $10,000, pero no sumas mayores como $50,000, debido a la falta de protección y seguridad frente a los brokers regulados por FINRA.

Los brokers online convencionales suelen enviar las órdenes de los clientes a market makers y proveedores de liquidez mediante acuerdos de flujo de órdenes. Este proceso puede tardar varios segundos o incluso minutos, lo que limita la rapidez de ejecución. Estos brokers se enfocan más en ofrecer investigación y análisis fundamental que en velocidad, por lo que son adecuados para inversores y swing traders minoristas, pero no para day traders.

En contraste, los direct-access brokers se centran en la velocidad y ejecución directa de órdenes. Usan software especializado que conecta a los traders con bolsas como Nasdaq y NYSE o con redes de comunicación electrónica (ECNs). Sus transacciones se ejecutan en fracciones de segundo, mostrando confirmación inmediata en la pantalla del trader.

Sin embargo, tienen desventajas: requieren conocimiento técnico, a menudo exigen volúmenes mínimos de operaciones, y algunos aplican tarifas por inactividad si no se cumple con ese volumen.

Los direct-access brokers suelen ofrecer sus propias plataformas de trading propietarias, pero también permiten usar plataformas externas enfocadas en velocidad y ejecución. Por ejemplo:

📊 Your Core Chart Setup
	0.	Candlesticks (white/red)→ Direct, fast view of price action and intraday sentiment. Candlestick patterns often show momentum shifts faster than lagging indicators.
	0.	Volume + Average Volume Line→ Volume confirms price moves. The average line helps you see whether a breakout/pullback is supported by stronger-than-normal activity.
	0.	9 EMA & 20 EMA→ Short-term momentum gauges. Crossovers and the “space” between them often guide entries/exits. Day traders often use the 9 EMA as a dynamic support/resistance intraday.
	0.	50 SMA & 200 SMA→ Longer-term context. Even if you’re trading intraday, these levels often act like magnets (support/resistance zones that institutions respect).
	0.	VWAP→ One of the most critical intraday tools. Tells you where the average participant is positioned. Stocks above VWAP = buyers in control; below VWAP = sellers in control.
	0.	Previous Day’s Close→ Key reference level. Market makers and algorithms often react at this price. Gaps relative to the prior close can drive strategy.
	0.	Last Sale Price (tape)→ Lets you see micro-momentum in real time. Useful for scalping and confirming whether bids/asks are being hit aggressively.

🎯 Support & Resistance (S/R) Levels

	•	How you’re using them:

	•	Intraday → Update as fresh highs/lows form, or when volume spikes at a certain level.
	•	Practical effect:Gives you “decision zones” where you plan entries, exits, and stop-losses.

Market Orders
	•	Se ejecutan de inmediato a cualquier precio disponible (“at ANY cost”).
	•	Compran al ask (lado alto) y venden al bid (lado bajo).
	•	Riesgo de slippage: el spread puede cambiar en milisegundos y terminar con un llenado muy malo.
	•	Ejemplo: esperabas comprar a $10.97, pero se movió a $11.15 → pérdida de 18¢.
	•	Los market makers y traders profesionales ganan dinero aprovechando estos malos llenados.

Limit Orders
	•	Una limit order fija el precio máximo que estás dispuesto a pagar (compra) o el mínimo que aceptas recibir (venta).
	•	Tú defines número de acciones y precio exacto.
	•	La orden aparece en el Level 2 (market depth) y espera ser ejecutada.
	•	No hay garantía de llenado:
	•	Si el precio se mueve más arriba (en compras) o más abajo (en ventas), tu orden puede quedarse pendiente.
	•	Solo se ejecutará si el mercado regresa a tu nivel.

Marketable Limit Orders (Mas recomendada)
	•	Son el tercer tipo de orden más importante para day traders.
	•	Funcionan como una mezcla:
	•	Se ejecutan de inmediato (como un market order).
	•	Pero con un límite de precio máximo o mínimo (como un limit order).
	•	Tú decides cuántas acciones quieres y el rango de precio aceptado.
	•	Ejemplo:
	•	Acción TEVA → ask en $34.77.
	•	Colocas una orden de compra en ask + 5¢ = $34.82.
	•	El broker intentará llenarte en $34.77, pero si el precio sube, podrá llenarte hasta un máximo de $34.82 (nunca peor).
	•	Ventaja → ejecución inmediata sin riesgo de slippage extremo.
	•	Estrategia común →
	•	Comprar en “ask + 5¢”.
	•	Vender en “bid – 5¢”.
👉 En resumen: las marketable limit orders combinan velocidad de ejecución con protección de precio, por eso muchos traders las prefieren para el day trading.

Stop Loss Orders
	•	Objetivo:
	•	Limitar pérdidas cuando una operación va en contra.
	•	Proteger ganancias cuando la operación ya está a favor.
	•	Cómo funcionan según la posición:
	•	🔹 Posición Larga (comprado):
	•	Se coloca un stop sell por debajo del precio actual.
	•	Razón: si el precio cae hasta ese nivel, indica que el movimiento esperado al alza falló → mejor salir antes de mayores pérdidas.
	•	🔹 Posición Corta (vendido):
	•	Se coloca un stop buy por encima del precio actual.
	•	Razón: si el precio sube hasta ese nivel, invalida la expectativa de caída → se cierra la posición para evitar más pérdidas.
	•	En DAS Trader Pro:
	•	Existen 4 tipos de stop orders (cada uno con diferente mecánica de activación y ejecución).

Trailing Stop
Concepto clave
	•	Un Trailing Stop es un stop loss dinámico que sube con el precio, pero no baja nunca.
	•	Se define un trailing amount (margen fijo por debajo del precio de mercado).
	•	Si el precio sube → el stop se ajusta hacia arriba manteniendo la misma distancia.
	•	Si el precio baja → el stop se queda fijo.
	•	Cuando el precio toca el stop → la orden se convierte en Market Order y ejecuta la salida.

Ejemplo práctico (del texto)
	•	Posición: 100 acciones de FB a $165.
	•	Colocas un Trailing Stop de $2 → stop inicial en $163.
	•	El precio sube a $167 → el stop sube a $165.
	•	El precio baja a $166 → el stop se mantiene en $165.
	•	Si FB cae a $165 → se ejecuta un market sell cerrando tu posición.
	•	Si sigue subiendo hasta $175 → tu stop siempre irá $2 por debajo (en $173).

Stop Range / OCO / Bracket Order:

Concepto clave
	•	Un Stop Range Order (también llamado One-Cancels-the-Other — OCO o Bracket Order) permite definir:
	•	Un stop loss → para limitar la pérdida.
	•	Un target price → para tomar ganancias.
	•	Cuando una de las dos órdenes se ejecuta → la otra se cancela automáticamente.

Hotkeys en trading profesional
	•	Profesionales no usan el mouse para entrar, salir o modificar órdenes → usan Hotkeys para eliminar retrasos.
	•	La velocidad es clave: muchas estrategias de day trading requieren ejecución en milisegundos.
	•	Hotkeys típicos:
	•	Comprar en bloques fijos (ej. 400, 200 y 100 acciones).
	•	Órdenes de compra: marketable limit order al ask + 0.05 → asegura ejecución rápida.
	•	Órdenes de venta: marketable limit order al bid - 0.05 → asegura salida inmediata aceptando hasta 5¢ peor que el bid.
	•	Hotkeys calculan automáticamente:
	•	El tamaño de la posición (para vender la mitad o todo).
	•	El precio de bid/ask al momento de ejecutar.
	•	Factor diferenciador: el uso correcto de hotkeys muchas veces separa a los traders ganadores de los perdedores.

Short Selling Restriction (SSR)
	•	Definición:
	•	La SSR se activa cuando una acción cae 10% o más respecto al cierre del día anterior.
	•	Reguladores y bolsas limitan las ventas en corto para proteger a los accionistas actuales.
	•	Cómo funciona:
	•	Bajo SSR, solo puedes vender en corto en el ask (esperando a que un comprador acepte tu orden).
	•	No puedes vender directamente al bid → prioridad es para los vendedores “reales” que ya poseen acciones.
	•	Resultado: si quieres hacer short, debes colocarte en la cola del ask y esperar.
	•	Propósito:
	•	Dar prioridad a los accionistas que ya tienen el papel y quieren vender, frente a los short sellers que buscan beneficiarse de la caída.
	•	Impacto en la operativa:
	•	No puedes usar marketable limit orders para entrar en corto bajo SSR.
	•	Las ventas en corto bajo SSR son más lentas y menos seguras de llenarse.

Level 2 (Market Depth)
	•	Proporciona visión intradía en tiempo real del flujo de órdenes.
	•	Es un indicador adelantado (leading indicator) → muestra intención de compra/venta antes de que ocurra la transacción.
	•	Otros indicadores como medias móviles o gráficos son rezagados (lagging indicators) → muestran info después de la ejecución.
	•	Muestra la lista ordenada de mejores bids (compras) y mejores asks (ventas) de distintos participantes (market makers, ECNs, etc.).

📌 Bid–Ask Spread
	•	Bid: precio que compradores están dispuestos a pagar.
	•	Ask: precio que vendedores piden para vender.
	•	Spread = Ask – Bid.
	•	Características:
	•	En acciones con alto volumen → spreads pequeños (a veces de 1 centavo).
	•	En acciones con poco volumen → spreads más amplios.
	•	En momentos de fuerte movimiento → spreads pueden ampliarse drásticamente (incluso hasta $2 en caídas o rallies violentos).

Stocks in Play and Risk/Reward Efficiency

	•	These stocks show predictable and frequent moves that can be exploited.

Identifying Stocks in Play
	•	Trading the right Stocks in Play helps retail traders compete with algorithmic programs.
	•	Stocks in Play may include:
	•	Stocks with fresh news.
	•	Stocks moving ±2% or more before market open.
	•	Stocks showing unusual pre-market activity.
	•	Stocks forming important intraday levels to trade from.

Importance of High Relative Volume and Alpha Stocks
	•	If a stock’s trading volume is not higher than normal, it is likely dominated by institutions or high-frequency trading (HFT) algorithms → best to avoid it.
	•	High relative volume stocks are valuable because they move independently from their sector and the overall market.
	•	Example: even if the Dow Jones or SPY are red (weak) or green (strong), these stocks can follow their own direction.
	•	Only a small number of stocks each day trade independently in this way.
	•	These are often called “Alpha” stocks, also known as Stocks in Play, since market and HFT activity cannot control them.

Fundamental Catalysts for Stocks in Play
	•	Stocks in Play are often triggered by the release of fundamental news either the day before or during the trading session.
	•	These news/events act as catalysts that drive significant price action.
	•	Common catalysts include:
	•	Earnings reports, warnings, pre-announcements, surprises
	•	FDA approvals/disapprovals
	•	Mergers, acquisitions, partnerships, major product releases
	•	Major contract wins/losses
	•	Restructurings, layoffs, management changes
	•	Stock splits, buybacks, or debt offerings
	•	Traders should check all stocks up or down more than 2% pre-market and create a Gappers watchlist.
	•	Stocks in Play can often remain active for several days after the initial catalyst.

Float, Market Cap, and Low Float Stocks
	•	Float = number of shares available for trading.
	•	Market Cap = total market value of a company’s shares (float × share price).
	•	Low float stocks (e.g., KOOL with 1.2M shares):
	•	Limited supply → high demand can cause rapid price moves.
	•	Tend to be volatile and move very fast.
	•	Most are under $10, usually early-stage and unprofitable companies.
	•	They often issue more shares over time, raising capital and possibly growing into large-cap stocks.
	•	Categories:
	•	Low float stocks under $10: extremely volatile (10%–1,000% daily moves possible).
	•	Often highly manipulated and very risky.
	•	Recommended only for experienced and well-equipped traders.
	•	The author personally avoids trading these stocks.

Trading Low vs. Medium Float Stocks
	•	Low Float Stocks (< $10)
	•	Best strategies: Bull Flag & Fallen Angel.
	•	Not suitable for most other strategies.
	•	Short selling is difficult since brokers rarely lend such volatile shares.
	•	New traders should avoid them: highly volatile, unpredictable, and hard to manage risk.
	•	Best left to professional traders.
	•	Medium Float Stocks ($10–$100, 20M–500M shares)
	•	More stable and suitable for day trading.
	•	Effective strategies: VWAP and ABCD Pattern.
	•	Stocks priced over $100 are less popular among retail traders because of their high cost and limited affordability.
	•	The author also avoids high-priced medium float stocks for these reasons.

Risk in Low Float Stocks ("Runners")
	•	In low float stocks ($1 per share), every 1 cent move = 1% fluctuation, making risk management extremely difficult.
	•	In comparison, for a $40 stock, a 1 cent move = only 0.025% fluctuation.
	•	Because of this volatility, low float stocks are much harder to trade safely.
	•	Traders often call them “runners” or, when reviewing past performance, “former runners”.

Consistency and Stock Selection for New Traders
	•	A major reason for inconsistent results in new traders is mixing different stock types.
	•	Pattern:
	•	Lose big on low float stocks.
	•	Win small on medium float stocks.
	•	Occasionally win big on a low float stock.
	•	This cycle causes portfolios to swing up and down, often leading to a gradual (or rapid) capital loss.
	•	Recommendation:
	•	Avoid low float stocks at the start of a trading career.
	•	Focus on building skills with medium float stocks, which are safer and more consistent.

Pre-market Gappers Scanner – Criterios
El escáner de gappers en el pre-market está configurado con las siguientes condiciones:
	0.	Gap: Acciones que en el pre-market hayan subido o bajado al menos un 2%.
	0.	Volumen pre-market: Que hayan negociado al menos 100,000 acciones antes de las 9 a.m.
	0.	Volumen diario promedio: Superior a 500,000 acciones.
	0.	Volatilidad (ATR): Tener un ATR (Average True Range) de al menos 50 centavos (es decir, cuánto se mueve en promedio una acción cada día).
	0.	Catalizador fundamental: Que exista un catalizador fundamental para el movimiento (ejemplo: resultados financieros, noticias, upgrade/downgrade de analistas, aprobación de FDA, etc.).

When there is a fundamental catalyst, there will be unusual pre-market activity and a Stock in Play will have gapped up or down before the market opens with a significant number of shares being traded (such as 100,000 shares).

I look for highly liquid stocks, so that buying and selling 1,000 shares will not be a problem. That is why I am looking at stocks with an average daily volume of over 500,000 shares.
I also am looking for stocks that usually move in a good range for trading. That is why I look at the ATR. As mentioned above, ATR means how large of a range a stock has on average every day.
If ATR is $1, then you can expect the price of the stock to move around $1 daily. That is a good number. If you have 1,000 shares, you may profit $1,000 from the trade.
But if ATR is only 10 cents, then that trading range is not attractive for me.

Pre-Market Selection and Buyout Gaps
When selecting stocks in the pre-market, two conditions must be present:
	0.	Clean and uniform price action,
	0.	Clear trading levels (support/resistance).
On the other hand, if pre-market price action is almost flat with no swings, even with high volume, it is not good for trading. This often indicates a buyout:
	•	The stock has gapped to the agreed acquisition price, but since the price is fixed, there will be no volatility during the day.
	•	Buyout gaps are common in the market, but they are not tradeable.
Key indicators of a buyout:
	0.	Flat pre-market price action, very close to the acquisition price (e.g., HAWK at $44.95 vs. acquisition price $45.25).
	0.	Heavy volume at a fixed price, usually representing institutional activity.

Circuit Breaker Halts and Scanner
What are circuit breaker halts?
	•	Triggered when extreme volatility occurs, often due to breaking news.
	•	Defined by SEC’s limit-up/limit-down rules: if a stock moves too far outside acceptable bands, trading is paused.
	•	Example: ~15% move within 5 minutes can cause a halt.
Duration:

Day trading relevance:

	•	Wild moves usually calm down after 2–3 halts.
	•	A Circuit Breaker Scanner helps traders by alerting immediately when a halt occurs, so they can prepare for the reopening move.

Methods of Finding Support and Resistance
	•	Traders use many methods to identify support and resistance.
Diagonal Trend Lines
	•	Very common among traders.
	•	Highly subjective: you can draw them differently depending on whether you want to go long or short.
	•	Example: the same chart can produce two opposite diagonal trend lines.
	•	The market does not “know” diagonal levels.
Horizontal Support and Resistance
	•	The only levels the author relies on.
	•	The market remembers price levels, not diagonal lines.
	•	Horizontal levels based on past price points are the most reliable.

What Are Support and Resistance Levels?
Support
	•	A price level where buyers step in or short sellers cover, stopping prices from falling further.
	•	Traders usually wait to buy at support.
	•	Short sellers also wait to cover at support, since they expect lower prices first.
Resistance
	•	A price level where sellers enter or previous buyers sell, stopping prices from rising higher.
	•	Traders sell here to secure profit before a possible reversal.
	•	Short sellers also add positions at resistance, expecting a drop.
Key Insight
	•	Old support can become resistance, and old resistance can become support.
	•	The role depends on the direction from which price approaches the level.

Bullish and Bearish Candlesticks
Day Trading Context
	•	Day trading is the study of mass psychology during the day.
	•	Candlesticks reflect the ongoing battle between buyers (bulls) and sellers (bears), showing who has the upper hand.
Bullish Candles
	•	Large-bodied candles to the upside = very bullish.
	•	They show that buyers are in control and likely to push the price higher.
	•	Candles don’t just show price—they reveal who has power in the market.
Signs of Reversal
	•	If the price gets very extended and candlesticks get smaller and smaller, momentum may be weakening.
	•	This is a potential sign of reversal (time to take profit or adjust position).
When to Stay Out
	•	If bulls and bears are equally strong, wise traders stay out.
	•	Wait for confirmation of which side is stronger before entering a trade.

Indecision Candlesticks
Dojis (Simple, Shooting Star, Hammer)
	•	Defined by having no body or a very small body.
	•	Called indecision candles because buyers and sellers have almost equal strength.
	•	Usually form with lower volume, as traders wait to see who wins.
Types of Dojis
	•	Simple Doji – wicks on top and bottom are about the same size → pure indecision.
	•	Shooting Star (top wick longer) – buyers tried to push higher but failed → suggests buyers are losing power, sellers may take control.
	•	Hammer (bottom wick longer) – sellers tried to push lower but failed → suggests sellers are losing power, bulls may take control.
Dojis in Trends
	•	In a bullish trend: a Doji can mean bulls are exhausted, and bears are fighting back.
	•	In a bearish trend: a Doji can mean bears are weakening, and bulls may step in.
	•	Important: Dojis = indecision, not a guaranteed reversal.
How to Use Dojis Effectively
	•	Don’t trade every Doji → this leads to losses.
	•	Always wait for confirmation candles.
	•	Best used together with support/resistance analysis and other indicators.

Candlestick Patterns
General Note
	•	Many candlestick patterns have fancy names but can be confusing or not very useful.
	•	Focus on simple, meaningful patterns that reveal market psychology.
Higher Highs and Higher Lows (HH & HL)
	•	Pattern of two or more candlesticks.
	•	The second candle’s high is higher than the previous high.
	•	The second candle’s low is also higher than the previous low.
	•	Shows that buyers are aggressive, making new highs, and sellers can’t push price lower.
	•	Very bullish trend signal.
Lower Lows and Lower Highs (LL & LH)
	•	The opposite pattern.
	•	Candlesticks form lower lows and lower highs compared to the previous ones.
	•	Shows sellers are stronger and buyers can’t maintain higher prices.
	•	Bearish downtrend signal.
Key Insight
	•	The color of the candlestick (red/white or green/red) is less important.
	•	What matters most is the relative positioning of highs and lows compared to previous candles.


Trading with Higher Highs / Higher Lows and Lower Highs / Lower Lows
Avoiding Wrong Entries
	•	Never go long when the stock is making lower lows and lower highs → bearish structure.
	•	Never stay short when the stock is making higher highs and higher lows → bullish structure.
Identifying the Uptrend
	•	An uptrend can be identified early, as soon as candlestick B closes, because A + B already form a Higher Highs and Higher Lows pattern.
	•	Professional traders enter after candlestick B closes → better entry and lower risk.
	•	Amateur traders often enter late (candlestick D or E), when the move is extended and risk is higher.
Misunderstanding the End of a Trend
	•	Many new traders think the uptrend ends at candlestick F.
	•	Reality: the uptrend continues until candlestick I, because the HH & HL pattern is still intact.
	•	A red candle alone does not end the trend if highs and lows keep forming higher.
Professional vs Amateur Behavior
	•	Amateurs may exit too early (e.g., at candlestick G after a red Doji).
	•	Professionals hold part of their position until the HH & HL pattern is broken (e.g., when a new 5-minute low forms).


Engulfing Patterns
Definition
	•	Engulfing Patterns involve two candlesticks, not one.
	•	Found often on 5-minute charts.
	•	Can be Bullish or Bearish.
Bullish Engulfing Pattern
	•	The new candlestick opens lower than the previous close.
	•	It then closes higher than the previous open.
	•	Shows a strong shift of power:
	•	At first, sellers push the price lower.
	•	Then buyers step in aggressively, reversing direction and pushing prices above the sellers’ starting point.
	•	Signals a major defeat for sellers/shorts.
Why It’s Bullish
	•	Buyers not only recover lost ground but overpower the sellers completely.
	•	Indicates strong buying momentum and a potential reversal.
Usage & Confirmation
	•	Considered a powerful reversal signal.
	•	Must always be confirmed with:
	•	Subsequent price action
	•	Other indicators (e.g., support/resistance, moving averages).
	•	If price later falls below the engulfing candle, the pattern may have failed.

Trade Management: The Real Difference Between Novice and Professional Traders
Two traders take the same trade using the same strategy.
	•	The first trader, fearing that the market will take away their profit, exits quickly and locks in a small gain.
	•	The second trader takes advantage of the pullback, adds to the position, and ends up securing a much larger profit.
👉 Same initial idea, but very different outcomes—all because of mindset and trade management style.
Novice Trader Mentality
	•	Believes trading is a passive process.
	•	Once in a position, they wait for either the profit target or the stop loss to be hit.
	•	They assume that nothing should be done after entry.
Professional Trader Mentality
	•	Understands that entering a trade is only the first step, based on limited information.
	•	As the market moves, new price action provides feedback on whether the initial idea is being confirmed or invalidated.
	•	Actively manages the trade: adjusts, scales in, or even exits early depending on how the market reacts.
Core Insight
The critical difference is not the entry strategy, but how the position is managed once it is open. Professionals treat trade management as a dynamic process, while novices treat it as something fixed.

Trade Management: Scaling and Adapting to Market Reactions
If you anticipate a break of a strong support level to the downside and want to profit with a short position, a smart approach is to start small:
	•	Begin by shorting 100 shares.
	•	When the stock makes a new low, scalpers and algorithms typically enter, aiming for quick profits of 5–10 cents.
	•	After they exit, the price often pulls back to retest the broken support, which now may act as resistance.
How to Manage This Trade
	•	If the retest holds below support (now resistance), you can add to your short position as the stock continues downward.
	•	If the retest fails (price goes back above support), you will get stopped out with only a small loss, since your initial entry was small (100 shares).
Broader Lesson
	•	Trade management is the most critical element in becoming a consistently profitable trader.
	•	It is also one of the hardest skills to teach, especially in books.
	•	Interestingly, in practice, even when two experienced traders take opposite positions (one long, one short) on the same stock, both can end the day profitable—because of how they manage their trades.

Trade Management: Taking Partial Profits and Protecting Gains
One of the most important trading rules is to take partial profits when a trade goes in your favor.
	•	You should typically sell 1/2 or 1/4 of your position to lock in profit.
	•	The remaining shares are left to continue moving in your direction, giving you the chance to maximize gains while reducing risk.
	•	Example: The author takes 1/4 of the position at the next profit target, using a Hotkey that automatically exits 1/4 of the remaining shares.
Two Core Fundamentals of Successful Trade Management
	0.	Never let a winning trade turn into a loss.
	•	Always secure part of your gains and protect yourself against reversals.
	0.	Move your stop loss in the direction of your trade.
	•	This practice, also known as a trailing stop, locks in profit while keeping you in the trade if momentum continues.

Trade Management: Protecting Profits and Avoiding Costly Mistakes
Locking in Early Profits
	•	Example: If your profit target is $1,000, decide that $250 must be protected.
	•	Once your open profit reaches $250:
	•	Close a portion of your position (e.g., 1/4).
	•	Move your stop loss to break-even.
	•	After this adjustment, you are essentially trading with “house money”: you have nothing to lose.
Scaling and Protecting
	•	After booking the first partial profit, decide what percentage of future gains you’ll protect.
	•	Example: Take another 1/4 profit at the next level.
	•	These percentages are flexible, depending on confidence and risk tolerance.
Common Mistake: Not Moving the Stop Loss
	•	Scenario: You go long, price rises, then pulls back into negative territory toward your stop.
	•	What should have happened?
	•	Stop loss should have already been moved up to break-even.
The Dangerous Temptation
	•	Traders often face the urge to lower the stop loss to “give the trade more room.”
	•	They justify it with thoughts like: “Just this once, I’ll make an exception.”
	•	This is a critical error:
	•	It violates discipline.
	•	It risks turning a small, manageable loss into a much larger one.
The Right Action
	•	When a trade starts acting badly, the correct choice is:
	•	Accept the small loss.
	•	Re-evaluate the chart.
	•	Be prepared to enter again if another opportunity arises.

Professional Trade Management: Flexibility and Reading Market Signals
Professional Trader Behavior
	•	Accept small losses quickly and stay alert for the next opportunity.
	•	Often take several small attempts (“quick stabs”) before catching the big profitable move.

Position Sizing – Summary
Importance of Position Sizing
	•	Position sizing is critical for consistency. Poor sizing leads to erratic results, regardless of how good the trade setup may be.
	•	Golden Rule: Never risk more than 2% of your account on a single trade. The priority is to preserve capital and live to trade another day.

Misconceptions of New Traders
	•	Many beginners believe they must trade with huge size to make meaningful profits.
	•	Reality: There is plenty of money to be made with modest size, especially in actively traded medium-float Stocks in Play.
	•	Trading with too large a size not only magnifies profits but also magnifies losses, often wiping out accounts quickly.

Experienced Traders’ Perspective
	•	Professionals sometimes take large positions, but only when the risk/reward ratio strongly favors it.
	•	The difference is that they can mentally and financially handle the risk.
	•	A bad loss at the Open does not paralyze them—they can reset, reassess, and continue trading.

Stock Characteristics and Sizing
	•	Medium-float stocks: Move more moderately → easier to manage risk → safer for larger positions.
	•	Low-float stocks ($1–$10): Can swing 10–20% within seconds → extremely risky → avoid taking large positions even if buying power allows it.

Practical Guidelines for Beginners
	•	Start with 100 shares:
	•	Low risk, small reward, but ideal for learning discipline.
	•	As you grow confidence in handling emotions and risk, increase share size gradually.
	•	Focus on developing skills, building the account, and scaling size only when ready.

Advanced Tactics – Scaling
	•	Many experienced traders do not enter their full position at once.
	•	Instead, they scale in:
	•	Start small.
	•	Add to the position only if the price action validates the trade idea.

👉 Key Takeaway: Position sizing is the backbone of risk management. Start small, respect the 2% rule, adjust size depending on stock characteristics, and consider scaling in only when the market confirms your thesis.

Scaling vs. Averaging Down – Summary
Scaling Up (Adding to Winners)
	•	The author rarely scales down into losing trades.
	•	If scaling is used, it is for adding to winning positions, not losers.
	•	Scaling is a double-edged sword: if used incorrectly, it becomes averaging down, which is dangerous.
Averaging Down (Adding to Losers)
	•	Beginners often confuse scaling with averaging down.
	•	Averaging down = adding more to a losing position to lower the average entry price.
	•	This is one of the most common and fatal mistakes for new traders.
	•	Works about 85% of the time → small wins.
	•	But the other 15% of the time → massive losses that can wipe out the account entirely.
	•	One bad trade is enough to end a trading career.
Key Lesson
	•	Day traders should not average down.
	•	Even if it seems profitable most of the time, the risk of catastrophic loss is far too high.
	•	Focus instead on scaling into winners, never losers.

👉 Key Takeaway: Averaging down is account suicide for day traders. Scaling should only be applied to trades already working in your favor.

Understanding Level 2 Signals – Summary
Role of the Montage / Level 2
	•	The Montage window (Level 2) is one of the most important tools in trading platforms.
	•	Most of the time, it doesn’t give clear answers, but occasionally it provides powerful signals to complement charts and indicators.

Key Level 2 Signals
	0.	Large Bid Order (big buyer on the bid):
	•	Surprisingly, this is often a bearish sign.
	•	Why? Because the buyer is waiting, not aggressive → price often drops.
	0.	Large Ask Order (big seller on the ask):
	•	Often a bullish sign.
	•	Why? Because the seller is sitting and waiting → price can move higher.

Psychology Behind It
	•	Aggressive buyers:
	•	Buy at the ask or above the ask.
	•	Signal urgency, possibly covering shorts.
	•	Willing to pay more → strong buying pressure.
	•	Passive buyers:
	•	Sit on the bid, hoping price comes down to them.
	•	Not aggressive → “low-balling.”
	•	Often forces price lower.
	•	Aggressive sellers:
	•	Hit the bid repeatedly → strong selling pressure.

Time & Sales (Tape)
	•	Shows where each transaction occurs:
	•	At or above the ask: aggressive buying.
	•	Between bid/ask: neutral.
	•	Below bid: aggressive selling.
	•	This reveals trader attitudes and urgency, which is critical for reading momentum.

Final Note
	•	A clear signal requires a very obvious, powerful bid or ask.
	•	Random or scattered bids/asks do not provide reliable signals.

👉 Key Takeaway: Large orders on Level 2 can be contrarian signals—a big bid can mean weakness, and a big ask can mean strength. The key is reading whether buyers/sellers are aggressive (hitting the market) or passive (waiting).

Fake vs. Real Level 2 Signals
NITF Orders (No Intention To Fill)
	•	Market makers sometimes mislead traders by placing fake orders.
	•	These are called NITF orders: they give the illusion of strong buyers or sellers but are never meant to be executed.
	•	How to recognize them:
	•	Real orders: placed close to current bid/ask → more likely to get filled.
	•	Fake orders (NITF): placed far away from current price (e.g., stock at $24.56 but ask at $24.98).
	•	They are quickly cancelled, especially once traders react.

Should You Trade Every “Signal”?
	•	No.
	•	With the rise of algorithms and high-frequency trading, signals can appear and disappear in seconds.
	•	Level 2 signals are useful but cannot be relied upon alone.

Proper Use of Level 2
	•	Always combine Level 2 with other indicators:
	•	VWAP position (is price above or below?).
	•	Daily chart (important support/resistance levels).
	•	Volume (confirmation of strength).
	•	Overall price action (candlestick patterns, trend).

Example: MOMO (March 7, 2018)
	•	MOMO was selling off below VWAP, looked like a short opportunity.
	•	However, large sellers on the ask were not aggressive → they were waiting for higher prices, showing confidence.
	•	Result: Instead of dropping, the stock squeezed up from $36.92 to $37.42 in just 40 seconds.
	•	The Level 2 also showed a clear NITF order at $38.95 (fake ask).

Lessons
	0.	Big orders are not always what they seem—distinguish between genuine and fake interest.
	0.	Never trade based on a single Level 2 signal.
	0.	Context is king: VWAP, daily chart, volume, and candlesticks matter more than one flashing bid or ask.

👉 Key Takeaway: Level 2 signals are powerful but deceptive if used in isolation. Always verify with other tools and remain alert to fake orders designed to mislead traders.

ABCD Pattern:
	0.	Identify the Stock
	•	Find a Stock in Play from my Gappers watchlist, one of my scanners, or a tip from the chatroom.
	•	When the stock surges up from point A and reaches a significant new high (point B), I wait to see if the price forms a support higher than point A.
	•	I call this point C. I do not enter the trade right away.
	0.	Watch Consolidation
	•	Monitor the stock during its consolidation period.
	•	Decide on my share size, stop loss, and profit target exit strategy.
	0.	Enter at Point C
	•	Once I see the price holding support at point C, I enter the trade close to the price of point C, aiming for a move toward point D or higher.
	•	Point C can also be confirmed on a 1-minute chart.
	•	It is important to use both time frames (1-min and 5-min) for better insight.
	0.	Stop Loss
	•	My stop is the loss of point C.
	•	If the price drops below point C, I exit and accept the loss.
	•	Buying close to point C minimizes risk.
	•	Some traders prefer waiting to enter at point D for confirmation, but I believe this reduces reward while increasing risk.
	0.	Take Partial Profit
	•	If the price moves higher, I sell half of my position at point D.
	•	I then raise my stop to my entry price (break-even).
	0.	Exit Remaining Position
	•	I sell the rest of my shares when:
	•	My target is reached, OR
	•	I notice the stock is losing steam or sellers are taking control.
	•	A new low on the 5-minute chart is a strong signal that buyers are exhausted.

ABCD Pattern – Beginner Summary
	•	The ABCD Pattern is one of the easiest and most popular trading setups. It works well because many traders use it, and following the trend is often the safest path in the market.
	•	How it forms:
	•	Point A → B: The stock makes a strong move up with buyers pushing it to new highs.
	•	Point B: The price is extended and risky. Don’t chase here—there’s no clear stop loss.
	•	Point B → C: Early buyers take profits, so the stock pulls back. Wait for the stock to hold at a support (Point C).
	•	Point C → D: If the price holds and then moves back up with high volume, that’s your signal to go long.
	•	Rules to follow:
	•	Never enter without a stop loss.
	•	Place your stop just below Point C (the support).
	•	Look for confirmation on both the 1-minute and 5-minute charts.
	•	Volume is key:
	•	You’ll often see big spikes in volume at Point B and Point D.
	•	Entry should happen when the breakout from C to D has significant volume.
	•	Example (Micron Technology – MU, March 12, 2018):
	•	Stock went from $56.38 (A) to $57.75 (B), then pulled back to VWAP and consolidated (C).
	•	A good entry was when it broke a new 5-min high above VWAP.
	•	Stop loss: below VWAP ($57.13).
	•	Partial profits were taken near the high, and the rest got stopped at break-even.

Bull Flag Strategy – Extended Summary
	•	Concept:
	•	The Bull Flag is essentially an ABCD Pattern, but most often applied to low float stocks under $10.
	•	The tactics are similar to the ABCD, but the difference is in duration and volatility: Bull Flags form and resolve much faster, making them harder to manage.
	•	Requires fast execution platforms and quick decision-making. Beginners are advised to avoid low float stocks early in their career.
	•	Pattern Structure:
	•	Resembles a flag on a pole:
	•	Pole = one or several large candles going up.
	•	Flag = sideways or slight downward consolidation (series of small candles).
	•	During consolidation, early buyers take profits, but price holds because new buyers enter while sellers don’t fully control.
	•	Many traders who missed the first move watch for this consolidation as an entry point.
	•	Entry & Confirmation:
	•	Entry occurs when price breaks above the consolidation area with heavy volume.
	•	Best confirmation is when a new 1-minute high coincides with a new 5-minute high, both with strong volume.
	•	Perfect entries are critical: bad entries create poor risk/reward ratios and often lead to losses.
	•	If the chart looks unclear or choppy, avoid trading it.
	•	Finding Bull Flags:
	•	Use scanners (e.g., Trade Ideas) to spot low float stocks making new highs of the day with heavy relative volume.
	•	The run-up (pole) phase usually aligns with these scanner alerts.
	•	Important: don’t chase a stock when it’s “running up.” Instead, wait for quiet consolidation and plan the entry.
	•	Risk Management:
	•	Stop loss is the breakdown of the consolidation area.
	•	Professional traders enter during quiet times and exit in volatility, opposite to amateurs who chase moves.
	•	Most scanner alerts are not tradeable: only about 5% offer solid risk/reward setups.
	•	Enter only during the first or second consolidation periods—later ones are risky because the stock is often overextended and buyers may lose control.
	•	Scalping Aspect:
	•	For low float stocks, the Bull Flag is often treated as a scalping strategy.
	•	Scalpers prefer entering at or near the breakout, not during consolidation, to reduce exposure time.
	•	The goal is to get in quickly when breakout is confirmed, take profit fast, and exit.
	•	These stocks can drop quickly and brutally, so risk must be minimized.
	•	Psychology & Philosophy:
	•	Patience is key—wait for the setup, don’t chase.
	•	Professionals profit by entering during low-volatility consolidation and exiting during volatility.
	•	Amateurs tend to do the opposite, chasing spikes and exiting too late.
	•	Momentum scalpers embrace the philosophy: get in on the breakout, scalp the move, and get out quickly.
	•	Variations:
	•	The Bull Flag is a long-bias strategy in an uptrend.
	•	A Bear Flag exists as the mirror image for short selling in downtrends, but often impractical due to lack of shares available to short in low float stocks.

	0.	Profit Target:
	•	The next important technical level, such as:
	0.	Important intraday daily levels that I identify in the pre-market.
	0.	Moving averages on a daily chart.
	0.	Previous day close.
	0.	If there was no obvious technical level for the exit and profit target:

Opening Range Breakout (ORB) – Beginner Summary
	•	What ORB IsThe Opening Range Breakout is a popular strategy that gives you an entry signal, but it does not tell you where to take profits. To make it a full trading strategy, you must always define your own profit target and stop loss.
	•	What Happens at Market Open (9:30 a.m. NY time)
	•	Stocks in Play usually show violent price action because of heavy buy and sell orders.
	•	If the stock gaps up:
	•	Overnight traders may sell to take profits.
	•	New traders may jump in to buy, expecting higher prices.
	•	If the stock gaps down:
	•	Some investors panic and sell immediately.
	•	Institutions may see it as a discount and buy large positions.
	•	This creates a battle between buyers and sellers. Smart traders wait and let the opening range form before making a move.
	•	The Opening Range
	•	Usually wait at least 5 minutes → the 5-minute ORB.
	•	Some prefer 15-, 30-, or even 60-minute ORBs.
	•	The longer the range, the less volatility, but the breakout signal is often more reliable.
	•	Works best on mid- to large-cap stocks, not on volatile low-float stocks.
	•	The range is defined by the high and low of the candlesticks in the chosen timeframe.
	•	Ideally, the opening range should be smaller than the stock’s ATR (Average True Range).
	•	How to Trade It
	•	When the stock breaks above or below the opening range:
	•	Go long if it breaks upward.
	•	Go short if it breaks downward.
	•	Stop loss:
	•	For longs → a break below VWAP.
	•	For shorts → a close above VWAP.
	•	Profit target: the next important technical level (daily levels from pre-market, moving averages on the daily chart, or the previous day’s close).
	•	If no clear technical level exists, exit if the stock shows weakness when long (e.g., new 5-min low) or strength when short (e.g., new 5-min high).
	•	Example
	•	A stock opened at $31, then sold off below $30 in the first 5 minutes after a 19% gap up.
	•	That showed sellers taking profit.
	•	After the first 5-minute battle settled, when the stock broke the 5-min opening range, the trade was to go short below VWAP.
	•	The exit was set at the next technical level, with a stop loss above VWAP.

👉 For beginners: ORB is all about waiting for the first battle to finish, then joining the winning side of the breakout with a clear plan (entry, stop, target).

VWAP False Breakout – Beginner Summary
	•	When it happens:
	•	Usually in the late morning (after 10:30 a.m.) and into the early afternoon.
	•	The idea:
	•	A strong stock with institutional buying usually trades above VWAP.
	•	If big buyers (like banks or funds) keep buying, the stock stays above VWAP and climbs higher.
	•	But if those buyers finish their orders or lose interest, the stock often falls back to VWAP.
	•	If it then drops below VWAP, it’s a sign of weakness → short sellers step in.
	•	The analogy:
	•	A stock that loses VWAP is like a buffalo running out of strength.
	•	Short sellers (the “wolves”) wait for the right moment to attack once VWAP is lost.
	•	Day traders “smell the blood” and short the stock when it breaks down under VWAP.
	•	Other scenario:
	•	If a weak stock below VWAP suddenly comes back up and breaks above VWAP, shorts are forced to cover (buy back).
	•	This creates a short squeeze → smart traders go long to ride the momentum higher.

AAOI – VWAP False Breakout Example (April 19, 2017)
	•	The stock opened weak, trading below VWAP toward the daily level of $43.61.
	•	It bounced off that level three times, then finally pushed above VWAP between 11:45 a.m. and 12:15 p.m.
	•	But AAOI was not strong enough to hold above VWAP or build an uptrend.
	•	It failed at the previous day close ($44.20), which acted as resistance, and then fell back below VWAP.
	•	This created a VWAP False Breakout → signal to go short around $43.75, with a stop loss above VWAP.
	•	Profit targets:
	0.	The new low of the day.
	0.	The next daily support level at $43.21.


To Summarize My Trading Strategy for VWAP False Breakouts
	0.	Once I’ve made my watchlist for the day, I monitor the price action around VWAP at the Open and during the morning session for the Stocks in Play.

	•	Buyers are gaining control.
	•	Short sellers may have to cover.
	•	However, if it loses VWAP again in the Late-Morning (10:30 a.m. to 12 p.m.), it means buyers are weak or exhausted.
	•	This situation provides a short opportunity, with a stop loss above VWAP.
	0.	The profit target can be:
	•	The low of the day at that time, OR
	•	Any other important technical level.
	0.	I try to go short when a Stock in Play has lost VWAP.
	•	Sometimes I go short before the price loses VWAP, to get a good entry as it ticks down toward VWAP in anticipation of a VWAP loss.

	•	If entering early:
	•	Take small size.
	•	Add more shares on the way down if you have truly identified a good trading setup.

VWAP Reversal Strategy – Beginner Summary
	•	What it is
	•	Stocks in Play often trade heavily at the Open. They may go to a new low of the day or stay strong above VWAP.
	•	Very often, they reverse and test VWAP. This setup is called the VWAP Reversal and can be traded long or short.
	•	How it works (example: JD.com, Nov 13, 2017)
	•	JD opened strong but sold off heavily below VWAP.
	•	At 9:50 a.m., it failed to make a new 5-min low → signal of reversal.
	•	This failure made short sellers nervous. When they covered, the price squeezed back up to VWAP.
	•	Trade idea: go long at $41.15, ride momentum back to and above VWAP.
	•	Why reversals happen
	•	If shorts expect the stock to keep falling but it doesn’t, they must cover fast → price shoots up.
	•	If the stock is above VWAP but fails to make a new high, the opposite can happen: sellers take over, giving a short opportunity toward VWAP.
	•	Signals of a reversal
	•	Failing to make a new 5-minute low (when below VWAP).
	•	Starting to make higher highs and higher lows on the 5-minute chart.
	•	Failing to make a new 5-minute high (when above VWAP).
	•	Risk management
	•	Always use a stop loss:
	•	If long → stop just below the low of the day or the last 5-min candle.
	•	If short → stop just above the high of the day or the last 5-min candle.
	•	Profit targets:
	0.	VWAP.
	0.	9 EMA or 20 EMA on the 5-min chart.
	0.	50 SMA on the 5-min chart.
	•	Best practice: take some profit at VWAP, move stop to break-even, and let the rest run.
	•	Important notes
	•	Not all VWAP reversals are tradeable. Many happen too fast with no good entry or risk/reward.
	•	It’s better to skip the trade than to enter late with bad risk.
	•	Even if you can predict the move, don’t trade without a good entry.
	•	Example Trade Setup (SIG)
	•	Long at $8.45 (failure to make new low).
	•	Stop loss below the low of the day.
	•	Targets:
	•	1st: VWAP (~$8.55).
	•	2nd: 9 EMA on the 5-min chart.
	•	3rd: 20 EMA on the 5-min chart.

VWAP Moving Average Trend
Bull Flags, the ABCD Pattern and Opening Range Breakouts happen at the Open. VWAP Reversals happen later in the Open and in the Late-Morning, and VWAP False Breakouts mostly occur in the Late-Morning session and into the early afternoon. But what happens to Stocks in Play during the rest of the trading day following the morning session?

The answer is that often Stocks in Play will find a trend and stay above the VWAP and move higher, or they will stay below it and move lower, using moving averages as their guide.

VWAP Moving Average Trend Trading
To summarize my trading strategy for VWAP Moving Average Trend trading:
	0.	When I am monitoring a Stock in Play and notice a trend is establishing around a moving average (usually 9 EMA) in the Late-Morning session, I consider VWAP Moving Average Trend trading.
	•	If the stock has already lost the VWAP (from a VWAP False Breakout), it most likely will stay below the VWAP.
	•	Similarly, if the stock squeezed above the VWAP in the Late-Morning session, it is most likely that it will stay above the VWAP, as it means the buyers are in control.
	0.	Once I learn that either 9 or 20 EMA are acting as either a support or resistance, I buy the stock after confirmation of moving averages as a support, but only if I can clearly see it “held” the VWAP.
	•	Similarly, I go short below the moving averages if I have the confirmation that it has “lost” the VWAP in the Late-Morning session.
	0.	I buy or sell short as close as possible to the moving average line (in order to have a small stop).
	•	My stop will usually be 5 to 10 cents below the moving average line or, if a candlestick, close below the moving average (for long positions).
	•	For short positions, a close above the moving average would stop me out.
	0.	I ride the trend until the break of 9 or 20 EMA.
	•	Usually, 20 EMA is a stronger support or resistance, so it is better to wait for that.
	0.	I usually do not use trailing stops and I constantly monitor the trend with my eyes, but I know that many traders also use trailing stops.
	0.	If the stock is moving really high away from the moving average, offering me an equally really nice unrealized profit, I may take some profit, usually at the 1/4 or half-position.
	•	I do not always wait until the break of moving average for my exit.
	•	Traders will say: “You can never go broke by taking good profits.”
	•	If the price pulls back to the moving average, I may add again to my position and continue the VWAP Moving Average Trend trade.
	0.	Remember, when you take profit, you should always bring your stop loss to break-even.
	•	Never go red on a stock that you already booked some profit on.

Having said that, a VWAP Moving Average Trend Strategy is an excellent trading strategy, because it usually does not require a very fast decision-making process and trade execution.

Trade Rules & Setups
If it doesn’t start working in the first 5 minutes of entering the trade, I often just bail out on breakeven. I expect immediate resolution from these patterns!Don’t get caught in those bull traps!

I will begin to sell if:
	0.	I see heavy resistance on the level 2 in the form of a big seller, I bail out by selling on the bid.
	0.	I stop seeing buying, and I’m just seeing a lot of selling on the time & sales, I bail out by selling on the bid.
	0.	If my first profit target has touched, I sell half by selling on the ask, and adjust stop to breakeven for remainder.
	0.	If 2nd or 3rd profit targets are hit, I continue to sell small pieces on the ask.
	0.	If my entry is on the 5-min chart, typically I will sell on the first red candle on the 5-min chart unless I’m already up so much that I can afford to hold through the pullback.

1. The First Pullback (1-min & 5-min)
The first pullbacks often take the form of the bull flag or flat top breakout.



Entry:The first candle to make a new high, the apex point of the bull flag / flat top pattern. Sometimes I’ll enter early to anticipate the breakout. If I’m entering early, what typically triggers that entry is either a break through a psychological level just below the apex point (half dollar/whole dollar), or a surge of buying on the level 2.
I would always prefer to have an early entry because it offsets the risk of a bull trap/false breakout.
Almost immediately upon entry I want to see the price moving up. If the price moves down immediately, my timing is wrong. If I have a starter position, I may hold with my stop at the low of the pattern and look to add near the support level of my stop, or add if the price returns to the breakout.
However, when I go red on a trade almost immediately, I often change my perspective to getting out at breakeven, either through adding near support and selling when it comes back out, or simply trying to exit at the price I entered at.
This is the type of trade that, when it works well, will work almost instantly, and the early entry allows me to start to accumulate a position as we see volume coming in. This is likely due to other trades also anticipating the breakout and perhaps short sellers covering as the stock shows increased strength.

The First Pullback Setup
Target:Target is a retest of high of day and a squeeze through the breakout price. Due to the inherent risk in any breakout trade of a false breakout, I will often take some profit into the first surge of momentum by selling on the ask a partial position and then adjusting my stop to breakeven on the balance. Sometimes when a stock holds through that first breakout, I will add back the position I sold for the next move up, and then again sell into that move up and continue adding and selling until the trend of pullbacks getting bought up changes.

Risk Factors:This is one of the lower risk gap-and-go setups. Stops are tight and these flag patterns are typically strong. Additional risks are secondary offerings coming out. I can check for an active S3, but of course many stocks have a Shelf Registration and do not do secondary offerings, so that doesn’t really guarantee anything. The company can also file a shelf on the same day and that would have a negative impact as it puts traders on notice that an offering may be coming.

Stop Loss:Stop can be low of last 5-min candle, or arbitrary stops at 5-10-20 cents.

Profit Target:10-20-40 cents. I’m typically going to use breakout of bailout rather than identify an exact to the penny exit. In fact, for most of my trades, although I may acknowledge the correct stop (technical based on the low of the previous 5-min candle or 1-min pullback), I don’t actually plan to hold all the way back down to that level.
If the stock doesn’t immediately break out and do what I think it will do, I’ll often try to get right back out at breakeven or for a small loss. The biggest problem with this approach is getting “chopped up.”
If a stock starts popping up 5 cents, dropping 7 cents, then back up 8 cents, I’ll find myself getting stopped out repeatedly. This is a stock we’d call choppy, and although I may end up red on it, my best trades are ones where I get in, and the trade works very quickly. If it’s choppy, I’m out.

Buying the Break of Pre-Market Highs

Entry:I enter based on the break of pre-market highs. If I take this trade pre-market, I’d be still buying based on the break of the current pre-market high, which is essentially a high of day breakout.
I like buying stocks at highs because when they are really strong, and they are the obvious stock in the market, traders are watching for them to break through highs. This is where short sellers cover and it’s where new long traders come in as the stock provides confirmation of its strength. This fits within the “buy high sell higher” attitude of many day traders.
As with all momentum and gap and go strategies, if I feel strongly that the stock will break through the highs and/or could make a large move higher (based on float, previous rate of change, former runner status, news, current market conditions, etc), then I’ll certainly try to anticipate the quick squeeze through the high by taking an early entry.
That entry may line up with:
	•	A half dollar or whole dollar, or
	•	An aggressive early entry as I’m seeing green on the tape and no sign of big sellers.
This setup will be typically the strongest on the leading gapper, and if the float is on the lower side (under 10mil).

Target
The target here is continuation and 15-25 cents of potential profit, depending on price of stock and market conditions.
	•	I look for a quick squeeze through the highs and instant profit.
	•	If the stock hesitates and is not giving me that instant winner, I may exit quickly to avoid being caught at the top for a false breakout.
	•	During a hot market I may increase my position through high of day. If I do that, I really need to adjust stops up so I’m not increasing my risk for loss on a false breakout.
👉 I’ll be more aggressive when the stock is the leading gapper, when the float is under 5mil shares, and when we’re in a hot market.👉 I’ll be more conservative when the opposite is true.
Be mindful that when the leading gapper fails, it can set the tone for the rest of the day.

Risk Factors
Anytime a stock is on the gap scanner, we know it’s extended, but this is one of my preferred ways to enter a strong stock. False breakouts are not uncommon.

Stop Loss
	•	Stop is the low of the last 5-min candle, or
	•	Arbitrary stops at 5-10-20 cents.

Profit Target
	•	10-20-40 cents.
	•	As with all gap and go trades, breakout or bailout will be important.
	•	Meaning if I get in and immediately it starts going the wrong way, I may simply exit rather than wait for the stock to come all the way down to the technical stop point.

Notes
This is probably the strategy I trade the most often, but false breakouts require me to take profits quickly, which is why my profit loss ratio is only about 1:1.

Buying Pre-Market Pivot

The Entry
Entry Price:We can visualize pre-market pivots as being very similar to ABCD patterns, but sometimes they are just not as clean in that they have taken much longer to form than the typical short period of a strong ABCD pattern.
But in any case, you will see a horizontal line on your chart based on a recent high, and visualize that if the stock can break that level, it should retest the pre-market highs.
The entry here can be a little tricky because:
	•	You can get in early to anticipate the break through this pivot.
	•	It may not be as obvious to all traders, which means the follow-through initially may not be as strong.
This is a setup where I’m typically more likely to:
	•	Take a starter position right at the pivot.
	•	Add into the nearest half / whole dollar if we see strength.
	•	Add through high of day.
👉 In a cold market, I may avoid this setup entirely.👉 In a hot market on a stock that is the leading gapper with a low float, I may trade it more aggressively.

Target
Target is a retest of the high of day, but expecting a big surge through high of day would only be common in a hot market.
This is a bit more of a base hit setup:
	•	Aim to generate a little profit while the stock starts to move back up.
	•	Acknowledge that the long period of consolidation below the pivot already indicates some level of weakness.

Risk Factors
The biggest concern here is that the stock is already showing some weakness because of the fact that it is stair stepping down a little bit.

Stop Loss
	•	Stop is the low of the last 5-min candle, or
	•	Arbitrary stops at 5-10-20 cents.

Profit Target
	•	10-20-40 cents.
	•	As with all gap and go trades, breakout or bailout will be important.
	•	If I get in and immediately it starts going the wrong way, I may simply exit rather than wait for the stock to come all the way down to the technical stop point.

Notes
This is another very common Gap and Go Strategy. In a lot of ways it’s buying a break of a flag pattern.

The Whole Dollar & ½ Dollar Setup



The Entry
Entry:Whole dollar and half dollar entries are a way of entering a strong gapper a bit earlier than the correct pattern-based entry might allow.
Example:
	•	If the first 5-min candle to make a new high is at 4.56, we may enter at 4.45–4.50, knowing that if the stock breaks the critical psychological resistance of the half dollar, it should shoot quickly to .56, giving us:
	•	An early entry
	•	An extra 5–10 cents of profit
Then, if it continues to show strength, I can add again at .85–.95 to anticipate a quick break through 5.00 (whole dollar).
⚠️ Be mindful: it’s not uncommon to see resistance at these levels. The only time I’ll be adding and adding is in a hot market on a stock that meets all criteria for having the potential to make a big move.

Special Situations
	•	When a stock is surging quickly (just broke out of a pattern, or had breaking news pre-market or intraday), an entry near a half dollar (e.g., 4.50) can be effective.
	•	If it breaks that level → stop goes right below it (4.48–4.49).
	•	If it immediately rejects → quick exit on breakout or bailout, usually out in the 4.30s–4.40s.
	•	During parabolic price action, I’m likely to take positions near half/whole dollars with stops on the other side of that critical level.
	•	(Discussed more in the momentum chapter.)

Target
	•	Typically continuation for a 15–25 cent move
	•	Most important: it must hold over the half/whole dollar entry point
	•	If it immediately rejects:
	•	That’s where short sellers want to hold short with a stop back above the half or whole dollar
	•	These prices can become a line in the sand for a tug of war
	•	What we want is a clean and convincing break

Risk Factors
	•	Half dollars and whole dollars = psychological resistance levels
	•	When they break → the break is quick
	•	Sometimes there’s a lot of buying but price doesn’t break → false breakout risk
	•	On higher-priced stocks, false breakouts can be dramatic
	•	Best used on stocks under $20

Notes
This is a great setup during a hot market, but I must be more careful during a choppy or slow market.

Opening Range Breakout Setup

The Entry
Entry:The opening range breakout is a strategy that works a bit better on stocks that have more of a grinding pattern and/or higher floats.
	•	The fact that the stock didn’t make a big move in the first 1–15 minutes of trading already indicates some sluggishness.
	•	The fact that it didn’t fall to create a red/green setup means short sellers aren’t likely to be trapped.
👉 This is not a setup I trade as often as the others, but it’s worth noting because occasionally we will see stocks churning in a choppy opening range, only to really open up when they break through that range.
Correct entry point:
	•	A break through high of day, which is the high of the opening range.

Target
	•	The target of a 1-min ORB is typically:
	•	A retest of the high of day, or
	•	At least 15–20 cents.
	•	Depending on overall market conditions and the characteristics of the stock being traded, expectations will be adjusted accordingly.

Risk Factors
	•	If the 1-min candle has a big range, getting stopped out or seeing a reversal is not uncommon.
	•	An additional risk factor is the inherent weakness in the fact that the stock didn’t rip right at the open.

Stop Loss
	•	The low of the opening 1-min candle, or
	•	Arbitrary stops at 5–10–20 cents.

Profit Target
	•	10–20–40 cents
	•	Breakout or bailout is still the rule of thumb.

Notes
	•	This is not a setup I trade a lot, however it can be good when it shows up.
	•	Sometimes the best gappers are too far off the pre-market highs to buy at high of day.
	•	In these cases, I wait for the first 1-min candle to close, and I:
	•	Buy the high of that candle
	•	Set stop at the low of that candle

Red to Green Setup



The Entry
Entry:This can be a truly powerful setup.
	•	This is where short sellers get trapped going short (perhaps on early weakness in the first few minutes of trading into a bear trap).
	•	Then, a sudden explosive rip through the open price and into the highs forces them to cover, while long traders jump in.
⚠️ Caution: I’ve seen this become a bull trap — it pops up through the open then immediately rejects and goes all the way back to the lows! If that’s the case:
	•	The stock is choppy
	•	It has a large range
	•	It’s understandable to feel frustrated
👉 However, when this setup works well, we can see very powerful moves through, as:
	•	Momentum traders continue to buy pullbacks
	•	Short sellers either continue covering into squeezes higher, or
	•	Sit on the sidelines feeling defeated

Target
	•	First, the stock goes through high of day
	•	Then continues through the pre-market highs
	•	The ultimate target will depend on the pre-market chart
	•	We want to see strong moves from this pattern

Risk Factors
	•	The fact that the stock already went red shows weakness → biggest risk factor
	•	Be careful of getting caught in a bull trap
	•	If you’ve accumulated a starter position to anticipate the red/green move, you’ll be in the best position — but this also means:
	•	You’ve been buying near the lows
	•	Without confirmation
	•	Which is risky

Stop Loss
	•	Keep tight stops since the early weakness is a warning indicator
	•	Stops at 5–10–20 cents

Profit Target
	•	10–20–40 cents
	•	Breakout or bailout!

Notes
This setup can work really well because early short sellers get stopped out as the stock squeezes through high of day.
⚠️ But remember: the early weakness is always a cause for caution.

For years, I would almost never trade pre-market. Sometimes we would see a big move pre-market and I would just accept that I missed it, but they weren’t a daily occurrence so I didn’t feel too bad.
In 2020, the market was extremely hot for momentum trading.

Eventually, stocks started making their big moves pre-market, and sometimes by the opening bell they were already fading back down!
At this point, I recognized the need to adapt to the current market and begin trading earlier on a more regular basis.

Success in Day Trading – Extended Summary
The Three Pillars of Success
	0.	Master proven strategies – Focus on one or a few strategies that you know work (e.g., ORB, VWAP setups, Fallen Angel).
	0.	Excellent risk management – Proper share sizing, clear entries, exits, and stop losses.
	0.	Control of emotions and psychology – Discipline is just as important as technical knowledge.

The Real Reason New Traders Fail
	•	Many beginners wrongly believe failure comes from lack of knowledge or strategies.
	•	They seek more indicators, new chatrooms, or other traders to follow.
	•	The truth: most failures come from poor discipline and psychology, not lack of information.
	•	Common problems:
	•	Impulsive decisions.
	•	Weak risk and money management.
	•	Lack of self-control.
	•	Core truth: You are both the problem and the solution in your trading career.

Trading Mindset
	•	“Live to play another day” – Protect capital, limit losses, survive to trade tomorrow.
	•	Don’t hold onto hope: “I’ll just give it more room.” Hope is not a strategy.
	•	Your goal is not to be right, but to make money.
	•	Every trade must have a defined stop loss level. If the stock invalidates your plan, exit.

Discipline Defines a Good Day
	•	A good trading day isn’t one with green P&L—it’s one where you followed your rules and stayed disciplined.
	•	Never turn a day trade into a swing trade by holding overnight.
	•	The hardest part for beginners: cutting losses quickly instead of waiting for break-even.

Risk Management – The Core of Trading
Purpose
	•	Risk management is the science and art of limiting losses.
	•	All traders lose money, even professionals.
	•	The difference between winners and blow-ups: controlling losses.

Two Key Elements
	0.	Risk/Reward Ratio
	•	Defined by entry, profit target, and stop loss.
	•	Only take trades with a ratio of 2:1 or better.
	•	Example: Risking $100 for potential $300 is acceptable.
	•	Example of poor trade: Entering short at $49.60 with target $48.80 (reward $0.80) but stop at $50.20 (risk $0.60).
	•	Ratio = 1.3:1 → not worth it.
	0.	Position Sizing
	•	Share size must match risk.
	•	Always limit loss per trade (e.g., 1% of account). → Keeps 99% of capital protected.

Stop Loss Placement
	•	Stops must be at real technical levels, not artificially close.
	•	Example: Stop loss below VWAP is meaningless if the stock naturally pulls back to VWAP before continuing.
	•	Use different timeframes:
	•	5-min chart may not show proper stops.
	•	1-min chart often gives better resolution for precise stop placement.

The Importance of Good Entries
	•	A correct trade idea isn’t enough. If the entry doesn’t give good risk/reward, skip the trade.
	•	Example with SQ:
	•	At 9:45 a.m., long at $41.75 with target $42.50.
	•	Stop at VWAP $41.00 → risk $0.75, reward $0.75 → 1:1 ratio → not worth it.
	•	Ten minutes later at $41.50:
	•	Target still $42.50 (reward $1.00).
	•	Stop at VWAP $41.25 (risk $0.25).
	•	Now risk/reward = 4:1 → excellent trade.

Core Lessons
	•	Discipline > being right. Your job is to follow rules, not to predict.
	•	Small losses keep you in the game. Protect account first.
	•	Risk/reward decides everything. Same trade idea can be bad or good depending on entry.
	•	Patience pays. Wait for the right entry that offers favorable ratios.

Risk Management Rules – Summary
The 2% Rule
Step 1 – Define maximum account risk per trade
	•	Never risk more than 2% of account on a single trade.
	•	Recommended for new traders: 0.5% to 1%.
	•	Example: With a $20,000 account (and $80,000 buying power), max loss per trade = $200 (1%).
Step 2 – Define risk per share
	•	Based on entry price vs. stop loss.
	•	Example:
	•	Short at $14, stop at $14.10 → risk = $0.10 per share.
	•	Long at $100.50, stop at $100.00 → risk = $0.50 per share.
Step 3 – Calculate position size
	•	Formula: Max account risk ÷ per-share risk = shares allowed.
	•	Example: $200 ÷ $0.10 = 2,000 shares max.
⚠️ Note: Calculating quickly is hard in live markets, but risk discipline is essential. Day trading isn’t supposed to be easy.

The 6% Rule (Dr. Alexander Elder)
	•	Do not lose more than 6% of your account in one month.
	•	If you hit –6% for the month:
	•	Stop live trading.
	•	Switch to a simulator for the rest of the month.
	•	Goal: Limit damage, focus on practice, avoid emotional spiral.

Practical Guidance for New Traders
	•	Start small – focus on executing correctly, not on making big profits.
	•	Don’t worry about commissions – at early stages, the goal is discipline and process, not net P&L.
	•	“First you get good, then you get fast.”

Gambler’s Fallacy
	•	Wrong approach: Gamblers increase bet size during losing streaks.
	•	Correct approach for traders: Reduce position size when account is dropping.
	•	Protect capital, avoid compounding losses.

👉 Key Takeaway:
	•	Risk management is survival.
	•	Limit each trade risk (≤2%), limit monthly losses (≤6%), calculate position size with discipline, start small, and avoid gambler’s thinking.

En esencia, la profundidad del mercado ayuda a confirmar soportes y resistencias, evaluar la absorción de órdenes y medir el sesgo del mercado en tiempo real. No obstante, hay que tener precaución: lo que se muestra en el Level 2 son intenciones (órdenes pendientes), no transacciones ejecutadas. Existe mucho juego y manipulación posible para engañar a quienes leen el DOM. Por ello, los traders profesionales suelen usar el Level 2 combinado con la cinta de tiempo y ventas (Time & Sales), donde se ven las operaciones realmente ejecutadas, para verificar qué órdenes del DOM se llegan a cumplir y cuáles podrían ser engaños

Spoofing: Órdenes Falsas que Buscan Engañar
¿Qué es el spoofing? El spoofing es una técnica de manipulación donde un participante coloca órdenes de gran tamaño con la intención de cancelarlas antes de que se ejecuten, con el fin de crear una falsa impresión de fuerte demanda u oferta En otras palabras, el spoofer muestra ordeñes fantasma – por ejemplo, un muro enorme de compra en el bid – para hacer creer a los demás traders que hay apoyo sólido y así empujarlos a comprar, solo para retirar repentinamente esas órdenes y potencialmente vender a un precio más alto Lo inverso ocurre en el lado de venta: mostrar un gran bloque en el ask para simular presión vendedora, incitando a otros a vender, mientras el spoofer en realidad busca comprar barato. Es una práctica considerada desleal e ilegal en muchos mercados (las bolsas de EE.UU. la prohíben), pero aun así puede ocurrir de forma sutil

¿Cómo identificar el spoofing en Level 2? Algunas pistas típicas incluyen: órdenes enormes colocadas en el mejor bid o ask que aparecen y desaparecen rápidamente sin llegar a ejecutarse, especialmente en momentos críticos de precio También cuando vemos que una gran orden se mantiene pero no hay correspondencia en la cinta (Time & Sales) – es decir, no se ven operaciones ejecutadas contra esa orden. Por ejemplo, un spoof común es colocar 100,000 acciones en el bid justo debajo del precio actual; si los vendedores atacan ese nivel pero la orden se quita justo antes de ser golpeada, es señal clara de spoofing. Los traders avispados también observan si tras quitarse ese “muro”, el precio cae rápidamente por falta de ese soporte artificial.

En bolsa, el spread es la diferencia entre el precio de compra (bid) y el precio de venta (ask) de una acción o activo en un momento dado.
📌 Ejemplo:
	•	Bid (lo máximo que alguien está dispuesto a pagar): $10.00
	•	Ask (lo mínimo que alguien está dispuesto a vender): $10.05
	•	Spread = $10.05 − $10.00 = $0.05
💡 Qué significa:
	•	Spread pequeño → mercado líquido, alta actividad, más fácil entrar/salir.
	•	Spread grande → menos liquidez, más costoso ejecutar operaciones.

Slippage
📌 Ejemplo
	•	Precio de compra (ask): $10.50
	•	Precio de venta (bid): $10.00
	•	Spread = $0.50
Si compras al precio ask ($10.50) y luego vendes enseguida al precio bid ($10.00), pierdes $0.50 por acción automáticamente, solo por la diferencia entre ambos precios.Eso es lo que se llama slippage en este contexto: la pérdida que ocurre al ejecutar una orden en un mercado con spread amplio.

1. Price: I perform best on stocks between $2-20, however I will trade outside this range occasionally
2. Percentage Change Today: I perform best on stock up 10% or more versus the prior days close. There are exceptions to this such as a continuation setup when a stock is gapping down slightly, but even in those cases, the stock should be up 10% vs the low of the day.

News: Earnings
When a stock announces its quarterly or annual earnings, reports this can be a big deal, especially if the results are much higher (or lower) than analysts and investors were expecting.

News: FDA or Clinical Trial Announcements
As active traders, pharma and biotech companies are some of our favorites. These stocks can go from zero to hero overnight when successful clinical trials on experiential treatments. We can also look for insurance companies to report approving the use of a certain drug to be covered by insurance.

News: Merger or Buyouts
Mergers or Buyouts can be tricky. If a stock has announced they are buying another company, the stock doing the buying will sometimes go down because investors fear the expense of the acquisition and the increased complexity of a buyout. The company being bought out will usually trade exactly at the buyout price, giving it no volatility and no opportunities for trading.

How a Float is Formed & Changes Over Time
	◦	Initial Public Offering: Issuance of the initial shares that become the float 
	◦	Secondary Offerings: When a company sells more shares onto the market through secondary offerings, direct offerings, and private placements to investors.
	◦	Stock Splits: When a company performs a traditional stock split the float increases. When a company performs a reverse stock split the float decreases.
	◦	Share Buybacks: When the company buys back shares.

Because an offering is generally bad for the stock price (that are typically dilutive), companies must put shareholders on notice that they intend to conduct an offering in the future. This is done via an S1 or an S3 filing with the SEC.

Moving Average Pullback (9 EMA or 20 EMA)

A moving average pullback has the following characteristics:
1) 3+ green candles moving up sharply followed by…
2) 2 or more candles of consolidation above the 9 EMA. This may start out as a bull flag or a flat top breakout, but then ends up having a false break, or consolidating for a longer period of time.
3) A perfect 5-min pullback pulls back to the 9 EMA on the 5-min chart. Sometimes these continue to fade all the way to the VWAP, and that means the new entry is at the support of the VWAP.
4) The entry point is not always extremely clear. Sometimes we start to see slight curling, and then take a 1-min bull flag after we see that volume is coming back. Stop will usually be 20 cents max.

No es simper first candle to make a new high aveces necesitas esperar un poco mas


================

An ABCD flag pattern has the following characteristics:
1) 3+ green candles moving up sharply followed by…
2) 2 or more candles of consolidation above the 9 EMA. Then a first 5-min candle to make a new high that DOES NOT break high of day. The high of this burst is point #2. Then a 2nd pullback to the 9 EMA, which forms point #3. The entry is when the stock moves back up for the 2nd time and breaks point #2. This becomes #4.
3) This consolidation should occur above the 9 EMA.
4) The entry point is a break of point #2. Stop will usually be 20 cents max.

A 1-min ABCD is Typically a 5-min Bull Flag which means Multi-Time Frame Alignment

The ABCD pattern is especially strong on a 1-min chart because it often means the 5-min chart is forming a bull flag. This means we have multi-time frame alignment, and both 1-min traders and 5-min traders will see this strong opportunity to buy the stock.

These are some of the most powerful setups.
A 5-min ABCD pattern can also be very powerful, but the 1-min chart will simply show a much longer period of consolidation, including a failed breakout at point B.

Bull Traps and Bear Traps

A bear trap / bull trap is when a pattern begins to resolve as you would expect, then quickly reverses. These quick rejections generally are unexpected and therefore catch traders off guard. People go long, only
to instantly get stopped out, or visa versa.
	
The best prevention for getting caught in a trap is to focus on stocks with high relative volume. However, even then, it will sometimes happen

A bull trap is when we see a bullish pattern resolve and then immediately reject and drop back down.

What can create a high likelihood of a bull trap?
1. The stock is well extended off the 9 EMA.
2. The stock has already made a very large move.
3. It’s anytime after 12PM.
4. The stock has already shown a tendency for false breakouts.

Traders who are FAST will be able to be profitable even during a false breakout. Traders who are slower and who took entries at confirmation will lose

There is no way to prevent getting caught in a bull trap from time to time. It will happen. The best remedy is to exit immediately, and to learn to see some of the red flags that can indicate a higher
likelihood of bull traps (such as previous history of false breakouts, extended chart, large sellers on the level 2, time of day, float, price, etc).

Bull traps can happen in any pattern: bull flags, flat top breakouts, whole dollar breakouts, VWAP breakouts, etc. Bear traps are the inverse for short sellers.

============
The Cup & Handle pattern is formed by a double top. The stock makes a first move up, pulls back more than you’d
typically expect, and then rallies all the way back to highs, forming a double top. Buying into a double top such as this
would be too risky. Most traders wait. Then a small pullback, in the form a bull flag, creates the handle of the pattern.
Entry can either be the break through the double top, or first candle to make a new high on the bull flag.

The market for each stock is like an island. You need a bridge to get to an island. In the market, there are many “bridges.” These are electronic pathways to the market called ECNs or routes.
Like bridges, some are free, and some charge a toll. “Express lane” would be high-speed routes and they often charge a small fee. Free routes are often slower.

A true market maker is a financial institution that owns shares of the stock and provides liquidity to the market by sitting on both the bid price and the ask price. They buy/sell shares across the spread to turn a profit. This is their compensation for the risk of holding the stock. They will use algo trading to generate profits.

An ECN can simply be an electronic pathway to the market such as NASDAQ, AMEX, NYSE.

Señales en el DOM (Level 2)
——————————————
Aquí “niveles” se refiere a niveles de precio en el libro de órdenes (Level 2/DOM), no a soportes/resistencias del gráfico.
	•	Nivel 1 (L1): mejor BID (mejor comprador) y mejor ASK (mejor vendedor).
	•	Nivel 2 (L2): el siguiente precio inmediatamente detrás del mejor.
	•	Nivel 3 (L3), L4, L5…: los siguientes escalones de precio en profundidad.

Alcista si:
Desequilibrio de libro (imbalance)
	Suma de los 3–5 mejores niveles de BID claramente mayor que los ASK.
 	Mejora si al mismo tiempo se retiran (pull) ofertas del lado ask.
BIDs apilados (“stacked bids”) justo bajo un nivel
	Varias capas de BID en escalera (p. ej. … .98, .97, .96) que no se rompen durante los test
	Si se “comen” parte del BID y vuelve (refresh), es aún más alcista.
Agotamiento del ASK (ask depletion)
	Ves cómo desaparecen niveles de ASK cuando el precio se acerca a una resistencia. Si se vacían 2–3 niveles en 1–3 s y el inside ask vuelve más alto → probabilidad de breakout.

Qué significan esos términos
	•	prints: ejecuciones en la cinta (Time & Sales).
	•	“prints al ASK” = compras agresivas (al que vende).
	•	“prints al BID” = ventas agresivas (al que compra).
	•	flip (del inside): el precio que era ASK (resistencia inmediata) se convierte en BID (soporte inmediato) y se mantiene unos segundos.
	•	stall: “atasco” o paro del avance. El precio deja de subir durante 3–5 s, reaparece oferta en el ASK y no salen nuevos máximos.
	•	pull del ASK: los vendedores del ASK retiran sus órdenes (la oferta se “vacía”/retrocede).
	•	refresh en BID: en un mismo precio de BID, te “pegan” muchas ventas pero el tamaño mostrado se regenera (hay comprador escondido/iceberg).
	•	ticks: mínimos incrementos de precio (p. ej., 0.01 en la mayoría de acciones).
	•	cruzar niveles de BID: barrer varios escalones de compradores de golpe.

Reescrito en español sencillo
1) Breakout con vaciado de ASK
	•	Condiciones: más tamaño en BIDs que en ASKs (imbalance a favor del BID), el ASK se retira (pull) y ves 5–10 ejecuciones al ASK seguidas.
	•	Entrada: justo arriba del nivel cuando veas el flip (el precio roto pasa de ASK a BID y sostiene).
	•	Stop: 1–2 ticks por debajo del nivel roto.
	•	Parcial: al primer stall (se para 3–5 s y el ASK reaparece).

2) Pullback a soporte con refresh en BID
	•	Contexto: el precio testea un soporte; se ejecuta volumen al BID pero el tamaño en ese BID se regenera (refresh).
	•	Entrada: en el segundo test fallido del soporte (la cinta marca mínimo más alto).
	•	Invalidación: si el refresh desaparece y barren varios niveles de BID de una.

3) Reversal por absorción
	•	Contexto: tras una vela roja fuerte, la caída se frena: muchos prints al BID sin que baje el precio y el ASK empieza a retirarse.
	•	Entrada: en el primer flip del inside a tu favor (lo que era ASK ahora BID y sostiene).
